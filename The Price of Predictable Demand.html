The Price of Predictable Demand

What would it cost to buy African-made vaccines at a time-limited price premium, and does that premium explain why demand remains uncommitted?

Neilay Bhalerao, MSc Control of Infectious Diseases (LSHTM). Written for the RVMC Secretariat. Unsolicited, and based on public data only.

The short answer

At the premium RVMC has already measured (41%), buying 30% of the eight vaccines in Africa's commercial pipeline from African manufacturers would add about US$95M a year. Allowing for uncertainty, that is US$48–267M. The base case is 18% of the US$530M a year announced for African manufacturing in 2022–24.

The premium is therefore a financing decision of manageable size, unless true African costs prove far higher than the evidence so far suggests. Two other results matter more. Even winning every dose of those eight vaccines would reach only about 45% of projected demand, short of the 60% goal. And 61% of the pipeline's volume is fill-finish, which leaves the drug substance imported.

Why this question

RVMC has named predictable demand the most pressing shared challenge. Its Vision asks directly, "What is the acceptable time-limited price premium for locally manufactured vaccines?" The January 2025 Lancet commentary asks how regions should "analyse, discuss, and agree" one. I reviewed the RVMC publications and articles and did not find that number answered, for any antigen or buyer.

The question is now live. Your June 2026 Nairobi call to action lists temporary price premiums among the market-shaping tools. Your July 2026 financing blog asks what makes demand credible enough to support investment. Gavi approved US$139M in July to buy African-made vaccines. A premium estimate by vaccine gives those conversations a shared starting point.

Method

For each vaccine: annual premium cost = 2030 African demand × reference price × premium × African share. This is deliberately simple, so that every input can be checked and replaced.

  • Demand: 2030 African demand for the 11 products across 8 antigens expected on the continental market by 2031 (CHAI 2026 Supply Landscape, using Linksbridge GVMM).
  • Prices: 2026 UNICEF awarded prices at the Gavi tier, the lowest price African buyers commonly face (Annex A).
  • Premium: 41%, RVMC Indicator 5.1, from 40 vaccines across regions. I tested it against Africa's one long-running price record (Finding 2).
  • Share: 30%, the level African Union heads of state asked Gavi to source from Africa in 2022.
  • Uncertainty: one-way ranges and a 200,000-draw probabilistic analysis. The ranges for premium (15–100%), share (15–50%) and demand (±20%) are my assumptions.

Analysis prepared with AI assistance (Claude) for document review, coding and drafting. Inputs, code and results are available on request and can be rerun in one command.

The eight vaccines are worth about US$771M a year at reference prices. A 41% premium on 30% of that is US$95M, roughly 18% of announced African manufacturing investment and 16% of estimated government vaccine spending in Africa (about US$606M, from Indicator 6.1a). As a yardstick only, AVMA's US$1bn over ten years averages US$100M a year. AVMA pays for milestones and supply, so it is a measure of scale here and not a proposed source of funds.

Gavi's new money offers a cross-check. US$139M for up to 70M doses implies about US$1.99 a dose, 29% above the basket's average reference price of US$1.54. The product mix is unpublished, so this is only indicative, and Gavi expects to spend it in the latter part of 2026–30 as eligible supply arrives. Volume is the sharper point: 70M doses is 14% of one year's demand for these vaccines, or under half of one year at a 30% share.

2. Africa's one long price record supports a premium near 35%

This is independent of the 41% figure and in the same range. It also shows why the comparator matters: in 2024–25 Dakar's US$1.533 sat at the bottom of Sanofi's quoted range. Dakar is a mature producer, however. New entrants at low volume could well need more, which is why the premium range runs to 100%.

3. The pipeline cannot reach 60%, at any price

Financing the premium does not close this gap. Reaching 60% needs at least 160M more doses a year from vaccines outside the current continental pipeline. CHAI also records no new continental commercialisation since 2024, with 10 transfers ongoing and 2 at regulatory filing. Decisions on the next antigens therefore have to be made soon, given the long lead times involved.

4. A premium buys different things depending on the vaccine

Drug substance would be made in Africa for only three vaccines: MR, yellow fever and cholera. They represent 39% of doses and 30% of value, and 85% of those doses rest on one institution, Institut Pasteur de Dakar, including an MR product not yet on the market. PCV, hexavalent and rotavirus account for most of the premium cost but would be filled from imported bulk, so they offer less protection if exports are restricted. A single premium figure hides this choice between industrial scale and health security.

Vial sizes differ, so these are first-order comparisons. They suggest that middle-income African buyers outside Gavi support could be a first market for some products. This also fits CHAI's finding that export prices below domestic levels constrain continental expansion. The missing piece is how many doses those buyers purchase, which leads to the first item in my proposed work below.

What this suggests for RVMC

  1. Answer the premium question by vaccine and buyer. Publish a premium envelope on the Hub, with one row per antigen and buyer segment, in place of a single median. It gives finance ministries a number to debate.
  2. Add a coverage ceiling to the Hub. Track the share of regional demand that current pipelines could serve, alongside Indicator 3.1a. It would have shown the 60% gap directly.
  3. Report end-to-end and fill-finish separately. Indicator 3.1a counts both as regional supply. Separating them shows whether growth adds security or only final-stage capacity.
  4. Sequence demand tools with product arrival. CHAI reports misaligned UNICEF tender and AVMA timelines. A dated map of tenders, approvals and product entry would show which windows new products would miss.

What this analysis cannot tell you

  • The true cost gap. I used observed price premiums. African cost of goods at realistic volumes is not public and could be much higher. This is the biggest unknown.
  • Who pays. The model sizes the bill without allocating it among Gavi, governments and other funders. Gavi's July 2026 Board paper records a US$111M gap between increased Vaccine Budget demand and new resources, even after counting the US$139M, so there is little slack.
  • Market effects. It holds global prices fixed. New entrants may push incumbents' prices down, and Gavi 6.0 policy is already creating demand uncertainty.
  • Costs beyond price. It excludes freight, financing, regulatory and supply-reliability costs, and any value from outbreak readiness.
  • Data limits. 2026 prices are applied to 2030 demand. Indicator 5.1 rests on limited data, and manufacturer and step details for the pipeline come from a CHAI slide.

Annex B: Inconsistencies I noticed in public materials

Offered in case they are useful before the next data refresh. Items 4 and 5 come from the data embedded in Hub charts, so they may differ from what a chart displays.

  1. Hub, Finance & Demand text gives LAC 29% and ASEAN 25%. The Hub chart and Status Report give ASEAN 29% (84M of 291M) and LAC 25% (136M of 554M).
  2. The Hub text and Status Report describe two exporting large manufacturers. The Hub chart says three exporters and marks Butantan as exporting, while the Methodology table marks Butantan as domestic.
  3. Status Report text gives about US$1.1bn a year for 2021–24. The dashboard and July 2026 blog give US$1.177bn for 2022–24.
  4. ASEAN WHO-prequalified vaccines: 11 in the Status Report, 12 in the Hub chart data and 6 in the Hub manufacturer table.
  5. The Hub's Africa priority-disease detail lists pneumococcal disease twice and omits meningococcal disease, which appears in the regional comparison table.
