Who Trusts a Latin American Approval?

The regulatory and political reach of vaccines made and approved in Latin America, prepared ahead of the Regional Vaccine Production session in Panama on 12 November.

Neilay Bhalerao, MSc Control of Infectious Diseases (LSHTM). Written for the RVMC Secretariat. Unsolicited, and based on public data only. Companion to The Price of Predictable Demand.

The short answer

A vaccine approved by the US FDA or the European Medicines Agency can use national reliance pathways in countries holding 86% of the region's births. An approval from Argentina's ANMAT reaches 17%, and one from Cuba's CECMED 16%. Brazil's ANVISA reached 22% until Mexico opened a pathway for it in 2026, which lifted its reach to 52%.

Most of the gap is a political choice written into law. Countries with 70% of the region's births already rely on foreign regulators but name only regulators from outside Latin America. Adding the region's PAHO reference regulators to those existing lists would raise the reach of each Latin American regulator to 80–87%, without building any new system.

Why this question, and why now

Thursday's session aims to identify barriers and take "a first step towards a shared roadmap" for regional manufacturing. RVMC and CHAI's May 2026 strategy for the region names limited market access as one of two structural risks. It calls for reliance and harmonisation "so that a single regulatory dossier can serve multiple markets". RVMC's Status Report adds that there is no systematic tracking of the laws that enable regional manufacturing, and Indicator 10.1 (political action) has no data.

This analysis puts a number on that market-access risk. It asks how many countries, and how many births, a vaccine approved by a Latin American regulator can reach through national reliance pathways, compared with one approved in the US or Europe. It focuses on registration law because that is where regulatory design and political choice meet.

Method

  • Who relies on whom: the 2021 study by Durán and colleagues in the Revista Panamericana de Salud Pública. It covers 20 regulators and 34 countries, using official documents from 2019–20, and names the foreign regulators each country trusts.
  • Updates since then: Brazil's reliance rule IN 289/2024, Argentina's Decree 892/2025 and Mexico's September 2026 ANVISA pathway. The Argentine and Mexican details come from secondary sources.
  • Market size: 2023 births from World Bank data, 9.31 million across 33 countries, as a proxy for routine vaccine demand.
  • Reach: the share of births outside a regulator's home country where national law allows reliance on that regulator's approval.

Reliance shortens registration but does not guarantee it, and laws written for medicines may treat vaccines differently. Treat these figures as the legal ceiling for an abbreviated route, not as approval times. Analysis prepared with AI assistance (Claude) for document review, coding and drafting.

What I found

1. The region trusts distant regulators more than its own

Thirteen of the 20 regulators studied accepted reliance in 2019–20, and every one of them named the FDA, EMA and Health Canada. Only seven also named PAHO's reference regulators: the Dominican Republic, Ecuador, El Salvador, Guatemala, Paraguay, Uruguay and the Caribbean Regulatory System, which covers 14 CARICOM states. Together they hold 16% of the region's births.

* Partial recognition: Mexico accepts ANVISA from October 2026. Colombia's abbreviated pathway covers OECD members, which include Mexico and Chile, but the source study does not count it as trusting Latin American regulators.

Brazil and Argentina have both widened reliance since 2020, and both named only regulators outside the region. So a vaccine made and approved in Argentina, such as Sinergium's, still needs a full national review to be registered in Brazil, and the reverse is also true. Mexico's 2026 decision to rely on ANVISA is the only reform I found that names a Latin American regulator.

3. A legal amendment would change the picture more than any new institution

This is a lever for finance and health ministers, not only regulators. Each country that already relies on foreign approvals could extend its list by decree or regulation. Bilateral steps such as Mexico and Brazil's show this can move faster than region-wide harmonisation, which is consistent with RVMC's view that progress can build from a few willing countries.

4. The Revolving Fund route is wider, but its fine print matters

PAHO's Revolving Fund accepts vaccines that are WHO-prequalified or approved by a functional national regulator for vaccines. Its published list includes ANVISA, ANMAT, CECMED and COFEPRIS. This matters because only 4 of the 36 LAC-made products counted in RVMC's Status Report have WHO prequalification. The Fund pooled about 60% of regional demand in 2022–24, according to the RVMC and CHAI strategy.

Two limits remain. Brazil and Mexico, with half the region's births, mostly buy outside the Fund. I also could not find a public, country-by-country statement of whether vaccines bought through the Fund still need national registration before use. That rule decides whether the Fund route really bypasses the reliance gap.

The strategy for the region already flags that PAHO's move from national regulators of reference toward WHO Listed Authorities "will introduce adaptation challenges". Argentina's exit adds an open question: how WHO-run assessments of ANMAT will work from now on. I could not find a public answer, and it bears directly on regional supply that depends on Argentine approvals.

Questions this raises for Thursday's panel

  1. For PAHO's Revolving Fund: which member states still require national registration for vaccines bought through the Fund, and could the Fund publish that list?
  2. For UNICEF Supply Division: how will prequalification work for products overseen by ANMAT now that Argentina has left WHO?
  3. For Gavi: as countries move out of Gavi support and buy for themselves, will their reliance lists affect which regionally made vaccines they can register?
  4. For the roadmap: could adding PAHO's reference regulators to existing reliance lists be an early, measurable commitment for each country?

What this analysis cannot tell you

  • Current law in every country. The baseline is from 2019–20. I updated Brazil, Argentina and Mexico only, and the Argentine and Mexican details come from secondary sources. Chile, Colombia, Peru and others may have changed.
  • Vaccines specifically. The source study covers new medicines. Vaccine rules, and exceptions for vaccines bought through PAHO or UNICEF, may differ.
  • The Caribbean. The source study says the Caribbean Regulatory System relies on PAHO's reference regulators, but does not name its designated trusted authority.
  • Practice. A legal pathway does not mean fast approval. Actual timelines would need regulator data.
  • Market size. Births are a proxy for routine vaccine demand. Adult and campaign vaccines follow other patterns.
